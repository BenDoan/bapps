      What BenFactor measures

      One number for how pleasant a city is to be outside in. Every month gets three scores — temperature, humidity and sky — which are blended 6 : 3 : 1, and the twelve monthly results are averaged. 100 is flawless; 0 is as rough as it gets.

      Temperature (weight 6), from the month's average daily high:

      • Base penalty: (daily high − 65°F)² ÷ 16
      • Above 85°F: an extra (daily high − 85)² ÷ 8
      • Below 45°F: an extra (45 − daily high)² ÷ 8

      The thresholds sit 20° either side of 65°F, so heat and cold ride the same curve: 72°F scores 97, 85°F and 45°F both score 75, 95°F and 35°F both score 31, and 100°F or 30°F bottoms out at 0. Only daily highs count, so overnight lows never drag a city down.

      Humidity (weight 3), from the month's average relative humidity:

      • Penalty: (relative humidity − 40%)² ÷ 25

      40% is the target, and being muggy or parched costs the same: 55% and 25% both score 91, 70% and 10% both score 64, and 90% bottoms out at 0.

      Sky (weight 1), from the month's average cloud cover:

      • Score: 100 − cloud cover %

      A month with a sky half covered scores 50. Clouds move the needle least, by design.

      The score is unitless and identical in °C mode, and all 202 cities carry all three inputs.

        Source: NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, U.S. Climate Normals (1991–2020), monthly, annual/seasonal and hourly normals, retrieved from the NOAA U.S. Climate Normals open-data archive. Every value on this page is baked into the page itself — no network calls, works offline.

        Humidity and cloud cover come from a second official archive, NOAA's Integrated Surface Database (ISD-Lite): every hourly observation each station reported from 1991 to 2020, averaged by hour of the day and then by month so uneven reporting cannot skew a month. Relative humidity is computed from those hourly temperature and dew point readings; cloud cover is the mean sky coverage in oktas.

        Normals describe a 30-year average for one weather station, usually at an airport, not a whole metro area.
